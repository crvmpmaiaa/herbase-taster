Pau d'arco · Ingredient notes

Pau d'arco. The inner bark is the whole point.

A South American tree, four common names, three botanical ones, and a bag of tea that is usually mostly the wrong part of the trunk. This is how I tell inner bark from outer bark across the counter, and what I am and am not allowed to tell you about either.

By Reiss Davies Ausar8 September 202610 minute read

A customer came in last Thursday with a kilo bag of pau d'arco tea and asked me why it tasted of nothing. She had bought it cheaply online and drunk it for a fortnight, wondering what the fuss was about. I tipped some out on the counter so we could both look.

Most of what came out was grey: hard curved pieces like broken roof tiles, corky and cracked on one face. Mixed through them were blond squarish chips, like something out of a hamster cage. In the bottom third of the bag was a handful of long reddish brown fibrous strips you could pull apart with your fingers. That handful was the pau d'arco. The rest was the outside of the tree and the wood under it.

This is the commonest problem with this herb by a distance. Pau d'arco is not bark, it is inner bark, and once you know what that means you can sort a good bag from a bad one in seconds. So this article is mostly about the inside of a trunk. Less romantic than the tree, and it decides whether you have bought anything at all.

01The tree

Pau d'arco comes from Tabebuia impetiginosa, a large deciduous tree of the family Bignoniaceae. That is the trumpet-creeper family, which also gives us jacaranda and catalpa. The flowers are long tubes flaring into five lobes, carried in heads at the ends of the twigs.

It grows across a broad band of South America: Brazil, Paraguay, northern Argentina and into Bolivia. Its country is seasonally dry rather than true rainforest, the cerrado and the drier woodland at the edges of the chaco, where the year splits into wet and dry. It is a big canopy tree, planted along streets and round plazas for what it does at the end of the dry season.

It drops every leaf and then flowers on bare wood. The crown turns magenta for a week or two, so completely that from a distance the tree looks like a pink cloud on a grey trunk. The Portuguese name for that colour form is ipê roxo, purple ipê.

The name pau d'arco is Portuguese for bow stick, and it describes the timber rather than the bark. The wood is one of the densest in commerce, heavy enough that a block sinks in water. Sold as ipe, it is the decking timber on boardwalks and terraces all over Europe.

That matters for supply. Nobody grows this tree in plantations for its bark, so the material in trade is wild-harvested, off trees felled for timber or stripped standing in the forest. A tree stripped in a ring round the trunk does not survive it. Bark taken in strips down one face, by somebody who means to come back to that tree, is a different proposition. Almost nothing reaching this country tells you which you have.

The bark is on the outside of a very valuable log, and nobody plants the tree for the bark.

02Inner bark, outer bark, and the bag on the counter

A trunk is built in layers. Outside is the outer bark, the dead corky armour that cracks and flakes as the tree expands. Under it sits the inner bark, a thin living layer carrying sugars up and down. Then a film of cambium, then the wood: sapwood first, pale and wet, then heartwood. Pau d'arco is the second of those layers and nothing else.

The inner bark is a strong reddish brown, somewhere between cinnamon and old brick, sometimes with a purplish cast on the inner face. It is fibrous along its length, so a strip of it does not snap. Pull at it and it shreds, splitting into long stringy threads with the grain, the way a stick of celery does. It bends before it tears. Chew a shred and it is bitter and drying, and the bitterness sits at the back of the tongue afterwards.

Outer bark behaves in the opposite way. Grey to grey-brown on the weathered face, thick, light for its size, and it snaps with a dry click rather than shredding. The fracture is granular, not stringy, and it tastes of very little. Sapwood is easier still: blond, near white, cut into hard square chips, and tasteless.

The reason a cheap bag is padded is arithmetic. The inner bark is a thin skin on a big trunk and separating it takes a person with a knife working down the log. Outer bark and sapwood come off the same tree in far greater quantity, cost nothing and mill beautifully. Cut material hides that well and powder hides it completely, which is why the bad bags are sold as tea cut or fine powder rather than strips.

Milled properly, the powder is a warm reddish brown with visible fibre in it, and light. Tip a spoonful into hot water and it stains quickly, running brownish red, the fibre staying suspended. A padded powder is pale fawn or greyish, heavier, and behaves like sawdust: it rafts on top or drops straight to the bottom.

The three-second versionPull a piece. If it shreds into fibres along its length, it is inner bark. If it snaps with a click, it is outer bark. If it is a pale square chip, it is wood. A bag that is mostly the last two is not pau d'arco with padding in it. It is padding with a little pau d'arco in it.

03Four names, and why the Latin keeps changing

This herb carries more aliases than anything else on my shelf, and they cause a lot of confusion.

  • Pau d'arco. Portuguese, bow stick, from Brazil. The name that stuck in the English trade.
  • Lapacho. Spanish, from Argentina and Paraguay, where the bark tea is domestic rather than a health-shop item.
  • Ipê roxo. Portuguese, purple ipê, after the flowers. Ipê is the name for the whole group of trees.
  • Taheebo. A trade name out of Brazil that travelled through North America. Mostly seen on older packaging.

Then there is the Latin, where one shelf will carry three binomials. Tabebuia impetiginosa, Tabebuia avellanedae and Handroanthus impetiginosus are, to all practical purposes, the same tree. Here is why there are three.

Tabebuia used to be an enormous catch-all genus holding around a hundred species of American trumpet trees. When botanists went back over it with wood anatomy and DNA sequencing in the 2000s it fell apart, and the hard-wooded ipês were moved into the genus Handroanthus, a name published decades earlier and then ignored. So Tabebuia impetiginosa became Handroanthus impetiginosus. Nothing about the tree changed. The filing cabinet changed.

Tabebuia avellanedae ends the same way. It was described from Argentina as its own species in the nineteenth century, and later work concluded it was the same plant, so it is now a synonym. Botanical naming runs on priority: the older valid name wins. Traders keep printing whichever name was current when they wrote their label, and labels get copied from labels.

Three names, one tree, and not three different products. The label to worry about is not the one using an old name, it is the one giving you no botanical name and no plant part. If a seller has gone to the trouble of separating the inner bark, they will say so on the front.

04How it is prepared

Traditionally this bark is a decoction, and I am describing a preparation here, not a purpose. In Paraguay and northern Argentina, lapacho is a kitchen tea: the bark goes in a pan of water, comes to a simmer and stays there a while before it is strained and drunk hot.

Simmering rather than steeping is the physics of the material, not fussiness. Leaves and flowers are thin and soft, so hot water pulls what is soluble out of them in minutes. Bark is dense, woody and largely insoluble, and water needs time and heat to work into the fibre. Pour boiling water over bark in a mug and you get faintly coloured water. Simmer the same bark in a covered pan and it goes the colour of strong tea with a reddish cast, and it tastes of something: bitter, tannic, drying at the sides of the mouth.

The capsule is a different object and I would rather say so than blur it. Dried inner bark, milled and weighed, in a vegan shell. No pan, no strained liquor, no taste, and the quantity is fixed rather than depending on how heavy-handed you were with the bark tin. You gain a measured amount you can carry and you lose the drink. I sell the capsule because most people will not stand over a pan every day.

05What I am allowed to say about it

Nothing. That is the honest answer, and it is worth setting out properly, because you are about to read a hundred pages that say otherwise.

I run a food business in England. The only health claims I may make are the ones on the Great Britain register, they attach to a named nutrient rather than to a plant, and I have to show the product holds enough of it to qualify. Every botanical claim in this country sits on a list that has been on hold for years. Pau d'arco is a bark: no measured nutrient figure to print beside it, no authorised sentence to attach to it. There is no health claim on this page and there is not going to be one.

On top of that sit the Human Medicines Regulations 2012: a product presented as treating or preventing disease is a medicine, and presentation alone is enough. Nobody has to test anything for that to bite. And an Act of Parliament from 1939 makes it a criminal offence to advertise anything as a treatment for one particular disease, prosecuted by Trading Standards rather than argued out with an advertising body.

Pau d'arco attracts some of the most aggressively illegal marketing in the whole category in this country. Not borderline, not over-enthusiastic. Pages that would fail all three tests above, written by people selling the same bark I sell. I am not going to repeat any of it, because repeating a claim in order to disown it is still publishing the claim. I have written sentences about this bark that I would not print today, which is part of why this site exists.

What I can tell you is what the thing is: the inner bark of a South American tree, wild-harvested, milled and put into a vegan capsule with nothing else in it. I can tell you how to check you have the right part of the tree, how it is prepared where it grows, what it costs and who should leave it alone. That is my side of the conversation. What it does, if anything, is yours.

06How it is taken

The listing prints the dose as two capsules daily, with food. That is what I would tell you across the counter, and I would not go past it.

Take them with a meal rather than on an empty stomach. This is a bitter, tannic bark, and bitter tannic things sit better on something. If you are drinking it as a decoction the same applies, and I would keep it to a cup rather than a jug.

The pack is inner bark in a vegan capsule. No fillers, no flow agents, nothing added, and nothing standardised either, because it is bark rather than an extract. It is £19.99.

One thing I ask that nobody else seems to: check what else you are taking. This bark turns up in blended formulas as well as on its own, including two of mine, so if you are already on something containing Tabebuia impetiginosa, read the ingredients before you add a second source of it. Doubling up by accident is easy and entirely avoidable.

07Who should not take it

This section is longer than the equivalent on most sites. I would rather lose a sale than skip it.

  • Pregnancy. Do not take it. Not in any trimester, not as a capsule and not as a tea. There is no safety data for pregnancy I would stand behind, and where there is no data the answer is no. If you are pregnant, trying for a baby or breastfeeding, leave this alone and ask your midwife or GP before taking any herb.
  • Anticoagulant and antiplatelet medication. Warfarin, apixaban, rivaroxaban, clopidogrel, daily aspirin. Speak to your GP or pharmacist first, and take the pack with you. I am not going to invent a mechanism, because I cannot source one I would be comfortable printing. This is a bark with far more unknowns than knowns and those drugs have very little margin in them, so it is a question for whoever manages your prescription.
  • Any prescribed medication at all. Same answer, less emphatically. Ask. A pharmacist will not mind being asked.
  • Surgery, dental extractions and any procedure with a date on it. Tell the pre-operative team everything you are taking, including this. They will normally ask you to stop supplements before a date they give you, and that instruction comes from them.
  • Bleeding disorders, or anyone told to be careful about bruising or bleeding. Not without your consultant saying yes first.
  • Children. No. Keep it out of their reach along with everything else in the cupboard.
  • Anyone under a specialist for anything ongoing. Put it on the list you take to your appointment. Herbs belong on that list.

Beyond the list: do not exceed the printed dose, do not decide that if two is the dose then four is better, and if a bitter bark does not agree with you, stop. That last one sounds obvious and is the advice people most often ignore.

None of this is medical advice. It is the set of questions I ask before I sell anyone this pack, and I would ask you the same ones.
