Shilajit · Ingredient notes

Shilajit. How to tell resin from tar.

Most of the shilajit sold online is cut with something, and some of it was never shilajit at all. The material has a set of physical habits that are difficult and expensive to imitate, so you can check a jar yourself with a glass, a kettle and a fridge. Here is the whole method, in the order I would run it.

By Reiss Davies Ausar8 September 202611 minute read

A man came into the shop on Saturday with a small black jar in his pocket. He had paid about twenty pounds for it on a marketplace and he wanted to know whether it was real. I put the kettle on. By the time the water had gone off the boil we both knew, and it was not the answer he wanted.

I get asked this more than anything else. Shilajit is cheap to fake and expensive to make properly, which always produces a market full of the fake version. Some of what is sold is not shilajit at all. Far more is real material cut with something that costs almost nothing.

The useful part is that the true material behaves in ways that are hard to copy, and every test in this article works at a kitchen table.

01What shilajit actually is

Shilajit is not a plant and not an animal. It is a dark, tarry, mineral-bearing material that seeps out of cracks in rock faces at altitude. It comes from the Altai, on the borders of Russia, Kazakhstan, Mongolia and China, and from the Himalaya, the Karakoram and the Caucasus. Ours is Altai.

The formation takes a very long time. Alpine plants, mosses and lichens grow in the thin soil that gathers in crevices, die back and are pressed down into the cracks. It is cold up there and there is little oxygen in the fissure, so over a very long period that plant matter is compressed and broken down, taking up minerals from the rock around it.

In summer the sun warms the face, that mass softens and works its way out through the crack, and it is collected off the rock. It appears in the same places year after year, so collecting it is local knowledge rather than prospecting.

A good piece of finished resin is near black with a bronze cast, and a fresh break shows a smooth glassy face rather than a crumbly one. What matters more than the range is what happened to it after it left the rock.

It is collected off a rock face by somebody who climbs to it. Everything after that is filtration.

02How it is purified, and where the quality is won

Raw shilajit comes off the rock with everything else that was on it: grit, sand, rock dust, plant fibre, insect parts. In that state it is not a food, and anybody selling a lump of it as raw or wild scraped is selling an unfinished ingredient.

Purification is simple in principle. The material is dissolved in warm water over several hours, until the soluble part is in solution and the insoluble part is not. That mixture is filtered, repeatedly, through finer and finer cloth, and what each filter catches is thrown away. The liquid is dried at low temperature back into a resin.

Filtration is where the quality is decided, because it is the only step that costs the producer yield. Every pass takes out more grit and more saleable weight with it. A producer who filters once through muslin keeps far more of what he started with than one who filters five times, and on a phone screen the two jars look identical.

You can feel the difference. Rub a smear the size of a match head between thumb and forefinger. Properly filtered resin is smooth and slightly tacky and draws into a thread before it breaks. A poorly filtered one grates: a fine sand catches on your fingertip, and that sand is rock. It is the commonest thing wrong with a cheap jar.

03Six tests you can run at home

Run these in order. The first two decide it and the rest confirm. You need a clear glass, water off the boil, a fridge and a pea-sized piece. Work in daylight, because half of this is colour.

  1. The water test. Drop the piece into about 200 ml of warm water and stir slowly. Not boiling, and not milk or tea, because both hide what you are looking for.

    True resin: it comes off almost at once, in ribbons of brown, and two or three minutes of gentle stirring takes it entirely. The solution is clear golden brown at the edges and near black in the body of the glass, nothing on the bottom, no film on top.

    Cut or fake: a piece still sulking on the bottom after five minutes, a cloudy grey solution instead of a clear brown one, an oily bloom on the surface, or clumps that will not break up. Maltodextrin and molasses dissolve readily enough, but the water goes flat and opaque, more gravy than tea held to the light.

  2. The temperature test. Put the jar in the fridge for half an hour, then try to bend a small piece. Warm that same piece in a closed hand and try again.

    True resin: cold, it is hard and brittle. Press it and it resists, press harder and it snaps rather than squashing, and tapped with a spoon handle it sounds like a stone. Warm, it goes soft within a minute and pulls into a long thread that thins before it parts.

    Cut or fake: it behaves the same at both temperatures. The binder most fakes are built on has no equivalent transition, so cold it is a workable putty and warm it is a greasier one. A wax-based one softens but smears instead of threading. Identical out of the fridge and out of your fist is enough for me.

  3. Flame behaviour. The theatrical one, and I would not decide anything on it alone. A very small piece on a metal skewer, a few seconds in a flame, by an open window.

    True resin: it does not want to burn. It bubbles, swells, blackens and gives off a thin acrid smoke, and it stops as soon as it leaves the flame. What is left is a little ash.

    Cut or fake: sugar, syrup or wax flares, holds its own flame out of the heat, drips, and smells of burnt caramel or a blown-out candle. Sweetness has no business in this material.

  4. The smell. Open the jar cold, warm a scrape on the back of your hand and put your nose to it.

    True resin: bitter, smoky and mineral. Wet stone, woodsmoke, old leather, a little tar, something faintly animal underneath. It is not a strong smell across a room. It is one you have to go and find.

    Cut or fake: sweet. Molasses, treacle, instant coffee, liquorice. Or nothing at all, which means a great deal of filler and very little material.

  5. The taste. Taste the solution from the first test rather than the raw resin.

    True resin: strongly bitter, smoky, faintly metallic, and it lingers at the back of the throat after you have swallowed. No sweetness anywhere in it. It never becomes pleasant, only ordinary.

    Cut or fake: sweet, caramel, or flat and gritty on the teeth. Grit is rock dust and you should stop drinking it. Sweetness means something was added, because nothing that seeps out of a rock face tastes of toffee.

  6. The sediment test. Leave the glass from the first test standing for an hour, then look at the bottom through the side of the glass.

    True resin: uniform top to bottom, at most a faint haze of fine particles that lift and disperse when you tip the glass.

    Cut or fake: a distinct layer on the bottom that slides in one piece like silt in a puddle. That layer is what the producer did not filter out, and it is the difference between five filters and a tea towel.

What none of this tells youThese tests are about identity and filtration. None of them says anything about heavy metals, and no home test can: lead has no taste, no smell and no colour in solution. That is answered by a laboratory report on a named batch, or not at all.

04What a good label prints, and what most do not

Before you buy a jar the label is all you have. This is what I look for.

  • The range, named. Altai, Karakoram, Gilgit: a specific range in a specific country. Himalayan on its own covers five countries, and a seller who knows where his material came from tends to say.
  • Resin or extract. Different products at different prices, and the label should not make you guess. Powder, extract or a ratio such as 20:1 means it is not resin.
  • A heavy metals statement attached to a batch. Lead, arsenic, mercury and cadmium, with a batch number and a date. A line saying lab tested with no document behind it is a sentence, not a test.

That last one I will not compromise on. This material is scraped off rock, it has spent a very long time against mineral seams, and it takes up what is around it, which in some places includes lead and arsenic. Adulteration has its own history of introducing metals as well. That is a purity and safety question rather than anything about what the product does, which is why I can write about it at length.

Most labels print none of it. Mine prints the Altai, the weight, the price and the words dual extracted and lab tested. It does not print a fulvic acid percentage and it does not name a batch certificate, and I would rather write that than print a number I cannot put a document behind. Ask me what testing sits behind the jar on my shelf and I will tell you what I have.

05Resin, powder, capsule, gummy

The same material is sold in four forms, and something is given up at every step away from the first.

One material, four forms
ResinThe purified material itself, nothing added and nothing carrying it. You can run all six tests on it. Sticky, awkward to judge by eye, and the only form you can check.
PowderResin dried onto a carrier, usually maltodextrin, rice flour or gum arabic, then milled. The carrier can be most of the weight and the label rarely says how much. The tests stop working, because the water is now telling you about the carrier.
CapsulesThat powder inside a shell. The smell and the taste go as well, and you pay for the shell and the filler. Unverifiable at home.
GummiesSugar or a sugar substitute, pectin or gelatin, flavouring and colouring, with a small quantity of extract. The flavouring is there to cover the bitterness, and the bitterness was your best check.

Each step makes the product easier to take and harder to check, and those are the same step. Buy resin: you can put it in a glass of water and see what you have.

06What is in it, and what I am allowed to say about that

The listing for my jar names zinc, copper, nickel, manganese, potassium, phosphorus, vanadium, chromium, cobalt, magnesium, carbon, humic acid and fulvic acid. Those are compositional facts and I will print them. What I will not do is tell you what any of them does once it is inside you.

Fulvic acid is the compound everybody names. It belongs to the humic substances, a family of dark, complex organic acids formed as plant material breaks down over long periods. The fulvic fraction is the smaller and more soluble one, soluble across the whole pH range and strongly coloured, and part of why a true resin gives a golden brown solution rather than a grey one. I stop there, because the next sentence in every article about fulvic acid is a health claim.

I sell food supplements in England. The only health claims I may make are on the Great Britain register, they attach to a named nutrient rather than to a plant or a mineral deposit, and the product has to contain enough of it to qualify. Every botanical claim is on hold. Presenting a product as though it treats or prevents anything makes it a medicine in law. So the number of things I am allowed to tell you shilajit does is zero, and that is the answer, not a technicality I am working around.

Every shilajit advert online says otherwise, for dull reasons. Most sellers are overseas, enforcement is complaint led and slow, and copy gets rewritten by people who read the last seller's. My own live listing does the same, which is worse, because I know better. It is being rewritten, and this article is part of that.

07How it is taken

The dose as my listing prints it: a pea-sized portion of resin, stirred into 300 to 500 ml of warm spring water until it has dissolved, once daily in the morning, or morning and evening. The jar is 50 g and the listing calls that roughly a two month supply, which works out at a little under a gram a day at the once daily rate.

Getting it off the spoon is the practical problem, and everybody has the same fight in the first week. Run the spoon under the hot tap and dry it before you go into the jar, or dip the loaded spoon into the warm water and stir, and it lets go in a few seconds. Never a wet spoon back into the jar. It does not need refrigerating: a cupboard is right, and cold only makes it harder to get out.

Warm water, not boiling. Milk, tea and coffee all work and all hide the sediment, so use plain water for the first few days of a new jar. The taste is strongly bitter and smoky with a mineral edge and it sits at the back of the throat. Honey or lemon will soften it, though honey covers the taste you would use to check the next jar.

08Who should not take it

This is the part I would rather you read than any of the rest, and I would sooner lose a sale than get it wrong.

  • Pregnant or nursing. No. There is no adequate safety information for pregnancy or breastfeeding for this material, and where the information is not there, the answer is no.
  • Anyone on prescribed medication. Speak to your GP or your pharmacist first, and take a photograph of the label with you. That applies to any prescription rather than a particular list, because a mineral-bearing material is not one defined compound and your prescriber should be the one to look at it.
  • Anyone with a condition affecting iron levels, or who takes an iron supplement. Speak to your GP first. The material is iron bearing, which is a fact about its composition rather than a claim about what it does, and it belongs in front of anybody already counting iron. I will not offer a mechanism, because I do not have one.
  • Children. No, and keep the jar somewhere they cannot reach it. It looks like something interesting.
  • Anyone who cannot see a source range, a batch and a heavy metals statement. Whoever is selling it, and however well it does on the six tests. They find grit and filler. They do not find lead.
  • Anyone holding raw or wild scraped material. That is an unfinished ingredient with rock still through it. It is not food yet.

None of this is medical advice. It is the set of questions I ask across the counter before I sell anyone a jar.
