Buying well · Reference

How to read a supplement label. The front is marketing. The back is the product.

People bring me pots to look at far more often than they buy anything, and the first thing I ever do is turn the pot round. This is that turn, written down: the front of the pack, the ingredients panel line by line, what the fillers are actually for, the cost per day arithmetic, and how to tell a claim that is allowed from one that is not.

By Reiss Davies Ausar8 September 202612 minute read

People bring me pots. More often than they buy anything, if I am honest about how a Tuesday on Smithdown Road actually goes. Somebody comes in with a tub a relative gave them, or one they ordered at two in the morning off an advert, and sets it on the counter to ask whether it is any good.

The first thing I do is turn it round. Nobody thinks much of that, but the turn is most of the job. The front of a pot is written by the person who wanted you to pick it up. The back is written by the person who would have to defend it if somebody official asked.

So this article is the turn, written down. By the end of it you should be able to read the back of almost anything on a shelf, mine included, and work out what is in it, how much of it there is, what it costs you a day and whether the seller is following the rules. Some of this will cost me sales, because it will send people to a cheaper pot that happens to be honestly built. I would rather that than take money off somebody who cannot tell the difference.

01The front of the pack

Start by noticing how little the front tells you. It has three jobs: get seen at arm's length, look expensive, and give you a reason to stop. None of those needs information, so mostly there is none.

  • A common name with no species after it. Sea moss, ginseng, turmeric, nettle. One common name usually covers several plants from different places at very different prices, and the cheaper one gets sold under the name of the dearer one. Until you know the species you do not know what you bought.
  • A milligram figure in big type. 1000 mg looks like a measure of strength and is nothing of the sort. It might be one capsule or a three capsule serving. It might be dried root, or an extract, or an extract plus whatever was mixed in with it.

Then the words. They carry the argument, and almost none of them mean anything you can check.

  • Natural. Says nothing about the plant, the dose or the process. Everything in every pot began as something that grew.
  • Pure. Sounds like a specification, works like a mood. If it were true of the pot, the panel on the back would already show it.
  • Premium. A price signal dressed as a quality signal, there to make the shelf price feel deliberate.
  • Wildcrafted. Gathered somewhere that is not a farm. Sometimes a real difference, sometimes a word typed into a design file by somebody who has never met a harvester.
  • High strength. Stronger than what. No reference point is given, which is the point. It borrows the authority of a comparison without making one.
  • Full spectrum. Usually means whole plant material rather than an isolated fraction, which is a real distinction. On a front of pack it is there to imply completeness.
Including on my own listingsPremium, wildcrafted and full spectrum all appear on Herbase listings I wrote myself in earlier years. They come off as the pages get rewritten, and this article is part of why. It is difficult to complain about a vocabulary while still using it.

02The ingredients panel, line by line

The panel is the only part of the pack that has to be true in a way somebody could test. Read it slowly, a line at a time.

The botanical name

A good panel gives the Latin binomial, genus and species, like Withania somnifera or Panax ginseng. The common name is not unique and the binomial is. Ginseng could be Korean, American or Siberian, and the third is not even the same genus as the first two. Sea moss could be a cold Atlantic plant taken off rocks or a farmed tropical one taken off a rope, a difference of species, of ocean and of several pounds a jar. A seller who paid for the dearer plant names it.

The plant part

Root, leaf, bark, seed, flower, fruiting body. These are different products from one organism, with different chemistry and different prices, and the panel should say which is in the pot. A root that took four years in the ground is not the leaves off the same plant. With mushrooms, look for fruiting body or mycelium, which are again two materials.

Extract or whole herb

Whole herb powder is the plant dried and milled, nothing else. An extract has been soaked in water or alcohol and the liquid driven off, leaving a concentrate. Neither is automatically the better buy. What matters is knowing which you have, because the same milligram figure means two different things.

Extract ratios

You will see 4:1, 10:1, sometimes 20:1. A 10:1 extract means ten kilos of dried herb went in and one kilo came out. That is a statement about yield, not about potency. It does not mean ten times as strong in any particular compound, because what ends up in that kilo depends on the solvent, the temperature and the time. A high ratio on its own is the front of pack number wearing a colon.

Standardisation

Standardised to five per cent of a named compound means the maker measured one marker and adjusted the batch until it hit that figure. Ginsenosides in ginseng are the usual example. It is real information and expensive to produce, because it needs a laboratory. It is also narrow: one compound, one number. A whole herb powder carrying no such figure is not worse for lacking one.

The order of the list

Ingredients are listed in descending order by weight, as they went into the mix. That one rule turns the panel into a ranking, and the ranking is free information most people walk straight past. If the plant on the front of the pot is fourth in the list, behind two bulking agents and a flow agent, the pot is mostly not that plant.

The order of the ingredients list is the cheapest information on the pack, and almost nobody reads it.

03Everything else in the capsule

Excipients are the ingredients in a pot for the sake of the manufacturing rather than for the sake of the buyer. I want to be fair about them, because the category gets shouted about by people selling the alternative, and shouting is not an argument.

  • Magnesium stearate. A lubricant. Powder has to move through a filling machine at speed without sticking to the metal. Without it a fast line jams and the fill weights wander.
  • Silicon dioxide. Finely divided silica that stops powder clumping and keeps it running dry. The same job as the rice in a salt cellar.
  • Microcrystalline cellulose. Purified plant cellulose, used to bulk a small quantity of material up to a volume that fills the shell, and to give a tablet something to compress.
  • Rice flour. The same bulking job in a more food-shaped material. It is cheap, which is why it is used and why it turns up where the plant is thin on the ground.
  • Maltodextrin. A starch derived carrier. Liquid extracts are often dried by spraying them onto it, so a good deal of what is sold as extract powder is carrier by weight.
  • Dicalcium phosphate. A mineral bulking agent, mostly in tablets, where it gives the press something firm to work with. It is also a source of calcium, so it is not inert.
  • Titanium dioxide. A white pigment that makes shells and coatings look uniform. The rules on it have moved in recent years and are not the same in every market, so I will say only that I do not use it.

Then the shell, which is an ingredient and should be named on the panel.

  • Gelatin. Animal collagen, usually bovine or porcine. Cheap and well understood, and no use to a vegetarian, a vegan, or anybody whose diet rules out one of those animals. A pack that says only gelatin has not told you which.
  • HPMC. Hydroxypropyl methylcellulose, made from plant cellulose. The standard vegetarian shell.
  • Pullulan. Made by fermenting starch with a microorganism. Also plant based, dearer, and chosen for how little oxygen it lets through.

None of that is sinister. Every item exists because somebody solved a real problem on a real production line, and a pot containing them has not cheated you.

The argument for a short list is simpler than the one the internet usually makes. A capsule holds a fixed volume, so every milligram of lubricant, carrier and bulking agent is a milligram of room the plant did not get. Of two pots that both say 500 mg, the one whose panel reads plant, shell, nothing else gives you more of what you came for. That is arithmetic about volume, and it holds whoever is selling.

What this means for my potsThe Herbase capsules are 800 mg of plant material in a vegan shell, with no flow agent, carrier or bulking agent, which is the whole reason I will spend a page explaining what those are. An empty list only means something to somebody who knows what would otherwise be on it.

04The dose, and the arithmetic nobody does

Two numbers get confused constantly. Capsule size is the weight of what is in one capsule. Serving size is how many capsules the label tells you to take at once. A pack shouting 1600 mg on the front might be two capsules of 800 mg. The front uses whichever number is larger, every time, so read the serving instruction before the milligrams.

Once you have the serving, the useful figure falls out in one division. Capsules in the pack, divided by capsules a day, gives days. Price divided by days gives cost per day. Two examples off my own shelf, so you can see it done.

The same sum, twice
Seaking, £39.9950 capsules of 800 mg. The label says two each morning, so the pack is 25 days, and £39.99 across 25 days is a shade under £1.60 a day. The pack prints 25 day supply on it, which is the same sum done for you.
Northern Soul, £34.99 or £50.00The gel comes in 300 ml at £34.99 and 500 ml at £50.00. One tablespoon a day is 15 ml, so the small jar is 20 days and the large one is 33. That is £1.75 a day for the cheaper jar and £1.50 a day for the dearer one.

The second line is the lesson. The jar that costs fifteen pounds less on the shelf costs twenty five pence a day more to use. Cheap pots are usually small pots, or pots with a three capsule serving where the next one along asks for one. The shelf price is the number designed to be compared. The cost per day is the number that describes what happens to you.

The sum also keeps a pot honest about itself. If the count divided by the daily serving does not match the supply printed on the label, somebody has not checked their own copy. I have that error on one of my own listings, where a fifty capsule pot is called a forty day supply at one a day. It is logged and being fixed, and I would rather point at it than hope nobody does the division.

05What actually has to be on there

A food supplement sold in this country carries a set of things by law, and a few more by ordinary practice. A pack missing a mandatory item is telling you something about the operation behind it.

  • The name of the food. What the thing is, in words, not the brand name.
  • The list of ingredients, in descending order by weight.
  • Allergens, emphasised within that list so they can be picked out at a glance.
  • The net quantity, as a count, a weight or a volume.
  • A best before date.
  • The recommended daily portion, and a warning not to exceed the stated dose.
  • The statement that food supplements should not be used as a substitute for a varied and balanced diet, which is why you have seen that sentence on every pot you have owned.
  • A statement that the product should be kept out of the reach of young children.
  • The name and address of the business responsible for the food. A postal address, not a web form.

Two more I would treat as expected rather than as certainly mandatory, because the rules on them depend on the product and I would rather hedge than be firm and wrong. Storage instructions are required where a product genuinely needs particular storage, which mine does, and get printed either way. A batch or lot number is standard practice on any properly run line and is what makes a recall possible, although where a date carries enough detail the marking rules can work differently.

The address testIf a pot has no postal address on it, put it down. That line is the seller saying where they can be found if a batch turns out to be wrong, and it costs nothing to print. A brand that will not tell you which building it works from has told you how a complaint will go.

06How to read the claims

This is the section that changes how you shop, so I will take it slowly.

In Great Britain there is a public list called the Nutrition and Health Claims Register. It sits under Regulation 1924/2006 as retained in GB law, and it is the complete set of health claims a food business is allowed to make. Complete is the word to hold on to. If a claim is not on the register it may not be made, and no form of words, footnote or disclaimer changes that.

Three things about the register do most of the work.

  1. Authorised claims attach to a nutrient, not to a plant. The register deals in named nutrients: iodine, iron, zinc, vitamin D. There is no authorised claim for a herb, as a herb. A claim can be made about a mineral that happens to be in a plant, and never about the plant itself.
  2. They carry the word normal. The authorised wordings are phrased around normal function, and the word is not decoration. It is the difference between saying a nutrient plays a part in how a body ordinarily works and saying a product will do something to yours.
  3. You have to have enough of it. A claim may only be made where the product contains a qualifying amount of that nutrient, and the figure has to be declared. No number, no claim.

Then there are the botanicals. When the register was assembled, thousands of claims about plants were parked pending assessment, and they are parked still. On hold is the official description, and it has held for well over a decade. So every claim you have read about a herb, on any website, is one nobody is currently permitted to make.

The tellA seller who names a nutrient, gives the authorised wording with the word normal in it, and prints the figure for how much of that nutrient is in a serving, is following the rules. A seller who writes that a plant supports, boosts, balances or fights something is not. It does not matter how carefully the second one is hedged. May help, traditionally used for, and customers tell us are the same claim with a hedge attached, and a disclaimer at the foot of the page rescues none of it.

Here is the one claim this shop is allowed to make, so you can see a compliant one in its natural state. Seaking declares 75 µg of iodine in every capsule, which is a measured figure I can stand behind, and on that basis: iodine contributes to normal thyroid function and to normal energy-yielding metabolism. Two capsules contain 150 µg, the adult reference intake. The nutrient is named, the wording is the authorised one, the number is printed beside it. That sentence is available on the Seaking page and nowhere else on this site, because nothing else I sell carries a measured figure that qualifies.

Two other regimes sit alongside the register. Under the Human Medicines Regulations 2012, a product presented as treating or preventing a disease is a medicine, and presentation on its own is enough. Nothing has to be tested. And the CAP Code treats a seller's blog, product pages and customer testimonials as that seller's marketing, so quoting a customer does not move a claim outside the rules.

This is why the Herbase pages read the way they do. I publish what is in the jar, where it came from, how much of it there is and what to do with it. What it does to a body is not mine to say, and it belongs to the person taking it. It costs me sales, and I know roughly how many, because I have watched customers read a competitor's page promising them an outcome and then read mine offering a species name and a milligram figure. I have decided that is a trade worth making.

07Where the real information lives

None of the above tells you whether a batch is clean. That information exists, it is just not on the pack.

  • A certificate of analysis. A laboratory document, specific to a batch, listing what was tested and what came back. A serious supplier has one for every batch and will send it to a customer who asks. A generic certificate with no batch number on it is a brochure.
  • Heavy metal testing. Lead, cadmium, mercury and arsenic. It matters most for roots and for seaweeds, which take up what is in the ground or the water around them. Ask whether it is tested, per batch, and by whom.
  • A batch number you can quote back. It ties the pot in your hand to the paperwork in somebody's office. Without it nobody can answer a question about your pot, only about the product in general.

And then the simplest tool of the lot, which people forget they have. You can ring a shop and ask. Which species, which part of the plant, whether the batch has been tested for heavy metals, and what the pot works out at per day. Four questions, two minutes. How the answers arrive tells you as much as the answers do.
