Anamu, and why it smells like that

Petiveria alliacea smells of garlic and onion, and the smell does not stay in the pot. That is the first honest thing anybody should tell you about this plant, and almost nobody does. Here is the plant, the sulphur chemistry behind the smell, how to read a pack, and the long list of people I would not sell it to.

By Reiss Davies Ausar8 September 202611 minute read

A man came into the shop in March holding a pot of anamu capsules out in front of him, the way you hold something that has gone off. He had bought them online, taken two with his lunch, and by four o'clock his wife had asked what he had been eating. He had eaten a cheese sandwich. What she could smell was the capsules.

Nobody had told him, and that is the part I mind. Anamu smells strongly of garlic and onion, and the smell does not stay in the pot. It arrives on the breath a few hours after a capsule, and taken daily for a fortnight it turns up in sweat. It is almost never printed on a listing, so people find out at work.

So that is where I am starting. I do sell anamu. I would rather sell it to somebody who knew what they were buying.

01What it is

Anamu is Petiveria alliacea, a slender perennial herb of the Caribbean, Central America and tropical South America. The species name gives the game away. Alliacea means smelling of garlic, and whoever named it had plainly crushed a leaf first.

It sits in a small family called Petiveriaceae, split out of the pokeweeds, which is why older books file it under Phytolaccaceae.

The plant is unremarkable to look at, which is part of the problem. It grows to about a metre on a thin stem that goes woody at the base, with oval pointed leaves running alternately up it in a plain mid green. The flowers are small and greenish white on a long thin spike, and the fruits carry backward-pointing barbs, which is how the plant travels: on the leg of an animal, or the hem of your trousers.

It likes shade and disturbed ground. Forest margins, roadsides, the strip behind a house where nothing has been dug over in years. In Jamaica it is a plant of the gully, which is where one of its names comes from, and it is naturalised well beyond its original range, into parts of West Africa.

The names change with the country. Anamú through the Spanish-speaking Caribbean and Central America. Guinea hen weed, and gully root, in Jamaica. Mucura in Peru and the western Amazon. Tipi in Brazil. Apacín in Guatemala. A listing that recites all of them and tells you nothing else is usually a seller who copied a description rather than sourced a plant.

Root and leaf are both traded and they are not the same material. The root is a long pale taproot that goes woody with age, and the smell sits heaviest in it. Cutting one will fill a room. The leaf is lighter in every sense: greener once milled, softer in smell, and cheaper by weight, because a plant grows new leaves all season and has only the one root. Stem is neither, and it is the cheapest way to make a kilo weigh a kilo.

02Why it smells like that

This is chemistry, and I am going to keep it to chemistry.

Cut an onion and what reaches you is not the smell of the intact onion. Undamaged tissue holds its sulphur locked up in larger, stable, odourless molecules, in one part of the cell, while the enzymes that act on them sit in another. Break the tissue and the two meet, and out of that meeting come small sulphur-bearing compounds that evaporate at room temperature. It is why the smell arrives at the moment of the cut and not before.

Petiveria alliacea does the same trick with different starting material. It is nowhere near garlic and onion botanically, and the lineages arrived at comparable chemistry independently. Crush a fresh leaf between finger and thumb and the smell is there inside a second.

The compound named on our listing is dibenzyl trisulphide, and it is worth knowing what that is, because the name gets thrown about online as though it were a trademark rather than a molecule. A benzyl group is a benzene ring with one carbon hung off it. Dibenzyl trisulphide is two of those joined by a chain of three sulphur atoms in a row. Compounds built to that pattern are common in plants that smell of garlic, and the class is called the organic polysulphides.

What I am allowed to tell you about it is what it is, where it occurs and why you can smell it. On the last: the nose picks up sulphur compounds of this kind far below the threshold it needs for most other classes of molecule, which is why a little plant material makes a smell out of all proportion to it. What it does once it is inside a person is not something I am going to write about, and section 04 sets out why.

Small volatile molecules leave a body by every route a body has. Nobody is surprised by that at a dinner table.

It is also what the man in the shop had found out. Small volatile molecules leave a body by every route a body has, the breath and the skin included. Anamu behaves the way garlic behaves, and it is better to know on the Sunday than the Monday.

03How to tell a good pack from a bad one

People send me photographs of packs. Most of what is wrong is visible on the back, assuming there is one.

Whole herb or extract

Two different products at the same shelf price. Whole herb is the plant dried, milled and put in a capsule, so 400 mg of it is 400 mg of plant. An extract has been through a solvent and concentrated, and it should carry a ratio: 4:1, 10:1, meaning the weight of raw herb that went in for each unit that came out. A listing that says extract and prints no ratio has told you nothing, and plenty of sellers use the word loosely when they mean milled powder.

Which part of the plant

Root, leaf, stem, or aerial parts, the trade term for everything above ground. A good label says which. One that says herb and stops at least admits to the whole plant. One that says nothing is usually attached to whatever was cheapest that month. Ask. Anyone who bought it properly can answer in a line.

What else is in the capsule

Rice flour, maltodextrin and cellulose bulk a capsule out so a small amount of herb still fills it. Magnesium stearate and silicon dioxide are flow agents, put in so the powder runs through the machine cleanly. None of them is sinister and all have to be declared, but they take up room that could have held the plant you paid for. A pack listing three of them before the herb has told you its proportions. The shell is either gelatin, which comes from animals, or HPMC, from plant cellulose, which is what a vegan capsule is. Ours are vegan capsules.

The species, and where it grew

The Latin binomial belongs on the pack: Petiveria alliacea, spelled properly. A pack that only says Guinea Hen Weed is leaning on a common name, and common names get shared between unrelated plants in every trade. Country of origin belongs there too. Ours is grown in the Caribbean and South America.

Why "100 per cent" means nothing on a front labelIt is a statement about proportion with nothing said about what it is a proportion of. One hundred per cent of the fill, of the capsule, or of the herb portion of a blend with three other things in it. Turn the pack over. The ingredients list is the part with legal weight behind it, it runs in descending order by weight, and it either names one plant and a capsule shell or it does not. No back, no claim, only a word.

Our own pack says 100 per cent Petiveria alliacea in a vegan capsule and nothing else. It does not print which part of the plant, and I would rather say so than have you assume.

04What is in it, and what I am allowed to say about that

Here is the honest position, and it is shorter than you might expect.

I run a food business in England. The only health claims I may make are the ones on the Great Britain register. They attach to a named nutrient rather than to a plant, they require me to show the product contains enough of it to qualify, and every claim ever submitted for a botanical is on hold. Anamu carries no nutrient at a level I could print a figure for and hang a register claim on.

So what am I allowed to tell you about what anamu does in a body. Nothing. Not a hedged version, not a traditionally used version, not a customers tell us version with a footnote. Nothing at all.

I will go further here than on other pages, because this plant has earned it. Anamu has more illegal marketing attached to it than anything else I stock. Search the name and you will meet claims that in this country would be an offence under the Human Medicines Regulations 2012 the moment they were published, and a smaller number that would be an offence under section 4 of the Cancer Act 1939, prosecuted by Trading Standards.

I am not going to repeat any of them, not even to knock them down, because repeating a claim is one of the ways of making it. The volume of it ought to make you cautious rather than curious. A plant that needs that much said about it is being sold on the saying rather than on the plant.

What I can give you instead is the specification. It is Petiveria alliacea, harvested in the Caribbean and South America, it smells the way I have described, and there are 50 vegan capsules in the pot at £29.99. The rest is yours to find out and yours to describe.

05How it is taken

The dose exactly as the pot prints it: take one to two capsules daily with a meal and 500 ml of spring water.

With a meal matters, and so does the water. Five hundred millilitres is a pint and a bit, more than people picture, and most customers who tell me a capsule sat heavily on them took it with a mouthful.

The pot says with a meal and does not say which meal. I would take it with dinner. Very little happens on the way down, because that is what a capsule shell is for, but there is a garlic note on the burp ten minutes later and the breath arrives some hours after that. Taken in the evening, the worst of the timing happens while you are asleep.

Keep at it daily for a couple of weeks and expect it in sweat as well. Not enough to clear a room. Enough that somebody next to you on the train notices something and cannot place it.

Store it dry, lid on, out of the light, and not next to anything you would rather did not smell of garlic. That includes tea.

06Who should not take it

This is the longest section here, and longer than the equivalent on any other page I have written, because anamu warrants more care than most of what I sell.

  • Pregnancy. This is the headline. Do not take anamu if you are pregnant, if you are trying to conceive, or if there is any real chance you might be. Do not take it while you are breastfeeding. That is not a hedge, there is no dose at which I would say otherwise across the counter, and it is the one line here I would ask you not to read past.
  • Anyone on prescribed medication. Anything you take daily, anything for something ongoing, anything at all. Speak to your GP or your pharmacist first, and take the pot in with you so they can read the label rather than work from your description of it. I am not going to invent a mechanism to explain why, because I cannot source one I would be willing to print, and a made-up mechanism is worse than none. If a medicine matters enough to have been prescribed, it matters enough to check before you add anything alongside it.
  • Anyone due surgery. Stop in good time before the date and tell your surgical team, along with everything else you take. Ask how far in advance they want it stopped, because they will have a rule and it is not the same everywhere. A team going into an operation needs the complete list.
  • Anyone on anticoagulant or antiplatelet medication, or anyone told they bruise or bleed more easily than most. Ask first. Same honesty as above: I will not hand you a mechanism, I will tell you to ask somebody with your notes in front of them.
  • Anyone being treated for anything at all. Ask before you start, and do not stop something you have been prescribed in order to take this instead. That happens, and it is the thing about selling this plant that worries me most.
  • Children. No. Keep it up high with everything else in that cupboard.
  • Anyone who has reacted badly to it before. If it did not suit you, it still does not.
  • Anyone who works in close quarters with other people. Not a safety matter, but it is real, and I would rather raise it than have you discover it in a meeting.

If you are in any doubt, do not take it, and come and ask me. None of this is medical advice and I am not qualified to give any. It is the set of questions I put to somebody across the counter before I will sell them this pot.
