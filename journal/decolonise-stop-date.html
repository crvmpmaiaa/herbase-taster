The bitter · Ingredient notes

Wormwood, and why Decolonise has a stop date

Decolonise is a bitter. Nine ingredients, and one of them is Artemisia absinthium. Wormwood is taken in short courses and then left alone, so the pack prints a limit rather than a subscription. Here is what is in the jar, what thujone is, why the gap is four months, and who should not take it at all.

By Reiss Davies Ausar7 September 20268 minute read

A man in the shop last month read the back of a Decolonise jar and asked me why I had printed an instruction that loses me money. It says take it for 25 days, then leave at least four months. He had worked out, correctly, that a jar is 50 capsules and two a day, so a jar is exactly one course, and then I am asking him not to come back until the spring.

The answer is wormwood, and it is worth a page of its own. This is the one formula on my shelf that is a course rather than a habit, and everything odd about the way it is sold follows from that.

01What a bitter is

A bitter is a category, not a promise. It means a preparation built out of plants that taste bitter, and it is one of the oldest shapes in both the European and the Caribbean herbal. Gentian, wormwood, angelica, artichoke leaf, quassia in the Caribbean, bitter melon across Asia. The drinks trade took the same plants and kept the same word, which is why there is a bottle of Angostura behind most bars and why vermouth is named after wormwood in German.

I am not going to tell you what a bitter does. That is not modesty, it is the law, and I have written elsewhere about the claims I am not permitted to make. What I can tell you is what is in the jar, what the plants are, where they grow and how the thing is meant to be taken. That is the whole of this article.

The words I am leaving outEvery version of this formula sold anywhere online is described in words a food business here is not allowed to use, and if you have shopped for one you have read them. I am not going to repeat them in order to disown them. The specification is on the product page and it is all I have.

02The nine

Seven botanicals, psyllium husk and activated charcoal. That is the list on the site and there is no tenth. Here they are in the order the label declares them, with what each one actually is.

The nine, in label order
Tabebuia impetiginosaPau d'arco, or lapacho. A forest tree from Brazil, Paraguay and northern Argentina that flowers pink before its leaves come in. The bark is the part that is usually sold.
Momordica charantiaBitter melon. A climbing gourd eaten as a vegetable across South Asia, southern China and the Caribbean. About the most bitter thing anyone eats on purpose.
Petiveria alliaceaGuinea hen weed in Jamaica, anamu across much of Latin America. A Caribbean and Central American herb that smells of garlic, which is what alliacea refers to.
Syzygium aromaticumClove. Dried flower buds from a tree native to the Maluku Islands, picked before they open. Also on Seaking's declared list.
Dysphania ambrosioidesEpazote. A strong smelling Mexican herb, cooked with black beans in Oaxaca and Puebla. Older books have it as Chenopodium ambrosioides.
Artemisia absinthiumWormwood. The bitterest plant in the European herbal, and the reason for the stop date. Section three onwards is about this one.
Piper nigrumBlack pepper. The fruit of a climbing vine, picked green and dried until it wrinkles and blackens. Kerala, Vietnam and Brazil grow most of it.
Psyllium huskThe husk of the Plantago ovata seed, grown mostly in Gujarat and Rajasthan and milled to a pale flake. It swells in water, which is why the protocol asks for a full 500 ml.
Activated charcoalCoconut shell burned and then treated with steam until it is full of pores. A declared ingredient, not a filler, and the reason for the two hour rule in section six.

Four of those nine are things you could buy in a good grocer's: clove, pepper, bitter melon, epazote if you know where to look on Lodge Lane. That is normal for a bitter, because the category was built out of the kitchen long before anybody put it in a capsule.

Bitter melon is the one in that pair you are most likely to have eaten. The one you are most likely to have in the cupboard already is black pepper, which is the next picture, and it does not start out black.

03Wormwood

Artemisia absinthium is a silver-leaved perennial from Eurasia and North Africa, naturalised here for centuries and growing perfectly well on waste ground in this city. It belongs to a genus with mugwort, tarragon and sweet wormwood in it, so you have almost certainly eaten a relative of it.

It is bitter in a way that is difficult to describe until you have had it on your tongue. A very small piece of leaf will take over your whole mouth and stay there for several minutes. Absinthe takes its name from it, vermouth takes its name from the German Wermut, which is the same plant, and both of those drinks exist because somebody wanted the taste and needed the alcohol to carry it.

Herbals have handled wormwood the same way for a very long time: small amounts, short courses, then stop. You will find that instruction in books centuries apart that agree on almost nothing else. It is not a plant anybody has ever treated as a daily habit, and the modern cautions attached to it are a tidied-up version of the same instruction.

04Thujone, and the figure I do not print

Thujone is a compound in the essential oil of wormwood, and it is the reason the plant is regulated rather than simply sold. It is one of the few plant compounds named directly in the food flavourings rules, which set maximum levels for foods and drinks made with Artemisia species. That is why the recipe for absinthe is a legal document as much as a culinary one.

So the obvious question is how much thujone is in a capsule of Decolonise, and the answer is that I do not know. Nobody has measured it for this formula, and a number I cannot stand behind is worse than no number at all, so the sheet says so in plain words rather than leaving a gap for you to fill in. The same goes for the milligram split between the nine and for any extract ratio. Eight hundred milligrams a capsule is what I can tell you, and I tell you.

What I print instead of a figure is a limit. Twenty-five days, then four months. That is the older and blunter instrument and it is the one I actually have.

One aside on the folklore, because somebody always raises it. The nineteenth century blamed thujone for a great deal it does not appear to have done, and later work has put most of what was wrong with absinthe drinkers down to the quantity of alcohol they were drinking. That does not make the cautions optional. They are older than the absinthe panic and they were arrived at independently of it.

Two a day for twenty-five days is one jar exactly. That is not an accident, and neither is the gap.

05Why the gap is four months

Fifty capsules, two a day, is 25 days. The jar is sized to the course, the course ends when the jar does, and then the pack asks for at least four months before the next one. Four months is the number on the pack and it is the number I keep to.

Do the arithmetic on that and it is two courses in a year, three only if the dates fall kindly. A shop that wanted to sell you the maximum would print no limit at all and let you keep buying, and plenty do. That instruction costs us money. We publish it anyway, because a bitter is not a daily habit, and I would rather tell you the limit than sell you four courses a year.

It also makes the product easy to judge from the outside, which I am in favour of. Ask any shop how long you should take something for. If nobody there will give you a number, that tells you what kind of shop it is.

06Who should not take it

This is the part I would read twice. Decolonise has a longer list of people it is not for than anything else I sell.

  • Pregnancy and breastfeeding. Do not take it. Wormwood is not taken in either, and that is the standard guidance, not a house rule.
  • Epilepsy, or any history of seizures. Do not take it. That caution follows wormwood wherever it appears and it applies to this jar.
  • Children. It is not for them. Keep it out of their reach.
  • Anyone taking medicine of any kind. There is activated charcoal in the formula and charcoal can reduce how much of a tablet is absorbed, the contraceptive pill included. Leave at least two hours either side, and speak to your GP or pharmacist before you start rather than after.
  • Anyone who wants to take it for longer than the jar lasts. That is not what it is. Two capsules a day, 25 days, then it goes back in the cupboard.

None of that is medical advice, and none of it is me hedging. It is the set of questions I ask across the counter before I will sell anyone this particular jar, and if you order it online I have no way to ask them, so they are printed here instead.

07How it is taken

Two capsules a day, with 500 ml of water. The full glass matters because there is psyllium husk in the formula and psyllium swells in water. Do not take more than two in a day, and if you miss a day, take two the next day rather than four.

Twenty-five days is the whole course, and the jar runs out on the same morning the course does, which I did on purpose so that nobody has to count. When the jar is empty the course is over. If you finish a jar at the end of September, the next one is the end of January at the earliest.
