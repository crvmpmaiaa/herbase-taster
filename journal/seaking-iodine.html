Sea moss · Label notes

How much iodine is in a capsule, and in a spoon.

Seaking prints 75 µg of iodine in every capsule and 150 µg in the two you take. The Northern Soul gel prints nothing at all. Here is what those numbers are measured against, why the jar carries none, and the one ingredient that sits on the capsule list and not on the gel's.

By Reiss Davies Ausar4 September 20268 minute read

Somebody asked me across the counter last week how much iodine was in a spoonful of the gel, and I told him I did not know. That is not the answer anybody wants to hear from the man who made the thing, and I gave it to him anyway, because the alternative was to say a number I had made up and watch him write it down. Then I put a tub of Seaking in his hand and turned it round, because that one I can answer to the microgram.

This piece is about the difference between those two answers, which is a bigger difference than it looks. It is the only measured mineral figure on anything we sell, the single number of that kind in the whole shop, so it is worth setting out properly. What the number is, what it is being held up against, why the jar sat next to it carries nothing at all, and what I am still not allowed to tell you even once the number is printed.

01The figure on the tub

Seaking is 50 vegan capsules at 800 mg each. The serving is two capsules in the morning with at least 500 ml of water, which makes the tub a 25 day supply and the arithmetic ends there.

Each capsule carries 75 µg of iodine. Two capsules carry 150 µg. That is the whole of the figure and there is nothing hiding behind it.

A microgram is a millionth of a gram. One hundred and fifty of them is a quantity you would not be able to sea if I tipped it out onto the counter in front of you, and iodine is one of the very few things anybody buys in a shop like ours where a number that small is worth printing at all. There is a reference intake for it and there is a published upper limit, and those two sit closer together than most people assume when they hear the word supplement. I am not going to print the upper limit on this page, because the figure I see quoted moves about depending on who published it and when, and you should be taking that one from your GP or from the NHS rather than off a man who sells seaweed in Liverpool.

02What 150 micrograms is measured against

A number on the back of a tub is only any use to you if you innerstand what it is being held up against. In Great Britain that yardstick is the nutrient reference value, the NRV. One figure per nutrient, set for a general adult population, and it exists so that a label can be read by somebody stood in an aisle with a basket on their arm rather than by a nutritionist with an afternoon free.

For iodine the NRV is 150 µg a day. So a serving of Seaking is 150 µg against an NRV of 150 µg, and one capsule is half of it. That is a tidy piece of arithmetic and it is the reason the figure is on the pack.

What an NRV is notIt is not a dose, it is not a target to hit, and it is not a permission. It is one population-wide reference number that exists so that one label can be compared against another label. Nothing about a food supplement changes what your own intake ought to be, and nobody who sells one for a living is in any position to work that out for you.

The listing also carries the figures for pregnancy and for breastfeeding, 220 µg and 290 µg a day. Those are higher, and they are still intakes rather than instructions to anybody. If you are pregnant or nursing, the person to ask about iodine is your midwife or your GP, and I would far rather you did that before you bought anything from us than after.

The other thing worth saying out loud is that a capsule does not arrive into an empty account. Milk, dairy and fish are the usual sources of iodine in a British diet, so whatever you take from us is landing on top of whatever you already ate this week without thinking about it. That is exactly why the figure gets printed rather than left for you to guess at.

03Why the jar has no figure

The gel is paused. Environmental Health has not yet certified the premises it is made in, so Northern Soul is off the shelf until that is sorted, not gone for good. Everything below still holds for when it is back, and it is the reasoning behind the empty row on its own specification sheet.

The Northern Soul gel is the same four algae as the capsules, soaked and blended with water, with nothing taken out of it and nothing dried off it. It is a good product, and it carries no iodine figure at all. It was not going to carry one either, and here is why.

Seaweed takes up iodine out of the water around it. How much of it ends up in the frond depends on the species, on the stretch of coast, on the season, on how long the weed sat before it was dried and on how it was stored once it was. Two harvests off the same rocks in March and in September are not the same material and never were. Mainstream science tries to understand the planet and the body, which are both water based organisms, without understanding water, and a wet gel is the place that catches up with you. A gel sold from a shop on Smithdown Road would need a laboratory number against every single batch before I could print anything at all, and that is not a cost a jar at £34.99 can carry.

So the tub says 75 µg and the jar says nothing. The same seaweed, and two different honesties about what we actually know. The gel's own specification sheet said exactly that in the iodine row rather than leaving the row empty, and it will carry on saying it once the gel is back, until somebody puts batch numbers in my hand that I can stand behind.

Dried, milled and weighed, the same seaweed sits still enough to measure. Wet in a jar, it does not.

05The clove that is on one list and not the other

Syzygium aromaticum is clove: the dried, unopened flower bud of an evergreen tree in the myrtle family, grown in Indonesia, Madagascar, Zanzibar and Sri Lanka. It is the same spice sat in your cupboard now, and the same one on the nine-ingredient list for Decolonise. A flower picked before it opens, dried until it rattles, and carried across the world for several hundred years.

It is declared on the Seaking list and it is not on the gel's. If you have ever held the two labels up next to each other and wondered, the difference is not a printing error. Four algae in both, and a spice in one of them.

What I am not going to do is tell you why it is in there. A formulator's reason for putting a botanical into a blend is the exact thing that becomes a claim the second it is written down on a page, and I would far rather keep the clove on the list than lose it arguing about a sentence. It is in there, it is named in Latin on the back of the tub, and you can smell it the moment you take the lid off.

06The sentence I am leaving out

There is permitted wording about iodine on the Great Britain register, and a serving of Seaking clears the threshold to use it, because that threshold is a proportion of the NRV and a serving here is the whole of it. We have printed that sentence once before. I am leaving it out of this one on purpose.

The reason is straightforward enough. A permitted sentence is still a sentence about a nutrient and a body, and the moment one of them lands on a page the rest of the page starts quietly leaning on it. Photographs get chosen to agree with it. Headings drift towards it a word at a time. Then somebody adds a second sentence that was never permitted at all, because it sounded like the first one, and by that point nobody left in the building can tell you which half of the page is legal. It happens slowly and it happens to good people.

What you get from me instead is the number and the yardstick, and nothing hung off either of them. 150 µg in a serving. 150 µg is the NRV. If you want to know what iodine is for, the NHS page on it is free, better written than anything I would manage on a Sunday, and not trying to sell you a tub at the end.

07The ceiling, and who should leave it alone

Two capsules a day is the ceiling printed on the pack and not an opening offer. I would rather lose the sale than have somebody work through a tub at four a day, so read this list properly.

  • Overactive thyroid. If you have hyperthyroidism, neither the capsules nor the gel are for you, because of the iodine.
  • Thyroid medication of any kind. Ask your GP or pharmacist first. If they are content for you to take something, this is the one where the figure is known, which is usually the point of the conversation.
  • Pregnant or nursing. Ask your midwife or GP. The reference intakes are different, 220 µg and 290 µg, and that is a conversation for a clinic rather than a counter.
  • Anything else you take that carries iodine. Kelp tablets, some multivitamins, some seaweed snacks. The figures add up whether or not you were adding them.
  • Both at once. If you take the capsules and the gel on the same morning, you are counting one and guessing the other, which defeats the reason for buying the counted one.
  • Children. Not for them. Keep the tub out of reach.

A missed morning is a missed morning and nothing has been lost. Take two the next day, not four.
