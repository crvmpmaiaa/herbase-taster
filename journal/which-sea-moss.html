Sea moss · Ingredient notes

Which sea moss? Two plants, one name on the jar.

Cold-water Chondrus crispus and warm-water Eucheuma cottonii both get sold as "Irish moss". They are different organisms from different oceans, and they behave differently in the jar. Here is how I tell them apart, and which one I would put in front of you for what.

By Reiss Davies Ausar2 September 20269 minute read

Somebody comes into the shop most weeks with a jar they bought online. Pale, thick, rubbery gel, "Irish moss" on the front, forty quid gone. They want to know why mine looks different. Mine is darker, thinner, and it smells like the tide came in. Theirs looks like a jar of hair gel.

The answer is that they were not sold the plant they thought they were buying. Nobody lied, exactly. The phrase "sea moss" covers several red algae, and the industry has quietly let one of them wear the name of another. So let me lay the two plants side by side, because once you have seen them next to each other you cannot unsee it.

01What sea moss actually is

Sea moss is a red alga. Rhodophyta, if you want the family name, and you should, because the family name is the first thing to look for on a label. Red algae are among the oldest lifeforms on the planet. They were photosynthesising in the ocean long before anything had crawled onto land, which is part of why they interest me. Everything we eat descends from something that grew in salt water.

Within that family there are two species you will actually meet in a jar in this country:

  • Chondrus crispus, the original Irish moss. Cold Atlantic water, the rocks off Ireland, Scotland, Brittany, Nova Scotia. Small, flat, fan-shaped fronds that fork and fork again. Deep purple to brownish red, sometimes almost black once dried. This is the plant the Irish were eating through the famine, and the plant every old herbal is talking about when it says "carrageen".
  • Eucheuma cottonii, the tropical one. Warm Indian Ocean and Pacific water. Grown on lines strung between stakes in shallow lagoons, in Tanzania, Zanzibar, the Philippines, Indonesia. Thick, cylindrical branches like a knobbly coral, pale gold through to purple. It grows fast, it grows big, and it is cheap, which is exactly why most of the world's "sea moss" is this plant.

There is a third, Gracilaria, which turns up in some Caribbean gel and gets called "Jamaican moss" or, again, "Irish moss". Same story: warm water, farmed, cheap. I am going to talk mostly about the first two, because those are the two I sell.

Most of the Irish moss sold in Britain has never been within a thousand miles of Ireland.

02How to tell them apart on the bench

I do this by eye and by nose. You can too.

Dried

  • Shape. Chondrus is flat and branching, like a tiny fern or a piece of fan coral, and the fronds are thin enough to see light through. Eucheuma is round in section, thick as a pencil, and looks like a cluster of stubby fingers.
  • Colour. Chondrus dries dark: purple, maroon, brown, near black. Eucheuma dries pale: straw, gold, off-white, sometimes a lilac tint. If a bag of "Irish moss" is golden and translucent, it is not Chondrus.
  • Salt. Wild-harvested Chondrus arrives with a fine crust of sea salt on the fronds and a proper smell of low tide. A lot of farmed Eucheuma is rinsed and sun-dried on tarpaulins and smells of very little.

As a gel

  • Chondrus makes a softer, looser, darker gel. Mine is somewhere between cranberry and mud, depending on the harvest.
  • Eucheuma makes a stiff, glossy, pale gel that holds its shape on a spoon. Very high in carrageenan, which is the thickening polymer the food industry extracts from it. That is the "hair gel" look.

On the label

The honest ones tell you the species. If a label just says "sea moss" or "Irish moss" with no Latin name, assume Eucheuma until shown otherwise, because nobody who has paid for wild Atlantic Chondrus forgets to mention it. Watch for "pool grown" and "aquaculture" too. Real Chondrus is not pool grown. It does not take to it.

Why the price is differentWild Chondrus crispus is picked by hand off cold rocks at low tide, in season, by people who know which rocks. Farmed Eucheuma is pulled off a rope in a warm lagoon every six weeks. One of those costs a lot more per kilo than the other, and the gap has to show up somewhere. If a jar of "Irish moss" is suspiciously cheap, it is being honest with you in the only way it can.

03What is in it, and what I am allowed to say about that

Sea moss is a mineral food. Iodine, magnesium, calcium, iron, zinc, selenium, a run of B vitamins, and a lot of the long sugar chains that make it gel in the first place. Between the two species the broad profile is similar. The differences are in the ratios and in how much carrageenan is carrying the rest.

Now, the part where I have to be straight with you. I run a food business in England. The only health claims I am permitted to make are the ones on the Great Britain register, and they attach to a named nutrient, not to a plant, and only when I can show the product actually carries enough of that nutrient. For the gel I cannot, because the iodine in a spoon of seaweed swings with the harvest and I do not print a figure I cannot stand behind. For Seaking, where every capsule is measured at 75 µg, I can. 04Gel or capsules

People ask this more than anything else. Honest answer: it depends what your mornings are like.

Same seaweed, two forms
GelThe whole plant, soaked and blended with water, nothing removed. Lives in the fridge. Goes on a spoon, in tea, in a smoothie, on your face if you want. Twenty-eight days once opened, or freeze it. This is the traditional way and the way I take it.
CapsulesThe same plant dried and milled, with a measured dose in every capsule. Travels. Sits in a drawer. Carries a printed iodine figure, which matters if you are counting. Loses the ritual, and the ritual is half of it.

If you are new to it and you are at home most mornings, start with the gel. If you are on the road, or you have a thyroid you are keeping an eye on and you want to know exactly what you took, Seaking.

05How I take it

One tablespoon, first thing, before anything else has gone in. Straight off the spoon, then a glass of water to chase it. That is the whole routine and it takes eleven seconds.

If you cannot stand the texture, and some people cannot, stir it into a warm tea with a spoon of raw honey until it disappears, or blend it into whatever smoothie you were making anyway. It will thicken the smoothie slightly. Soup works too, blended in at the end.

One spoon is the dose. Not two because you missed yesterday. The seaweed carries iodine, and iodine is one of the few nutrients with a real ceiling. Miss a day, you missed a day.

The plain Chondrus gel also goes on skin and hair. Leave it on the face or the scalp for up to an hour, rinse with water only, pat dry gently. I put that on the jar because customers kept telling me they were doing it anyway.

06Why your gel went watery, and how to store it

Three reasons, usually in this order.

  1. A dirty spoon. Anything that has been in your mouth or in another jar brings its own bacteria into a wet, mineral-rich gel. It will start breaking down within days. Clean, dry spoon, every time. It sounds fussy and it is the whole thing.
  2. Warmth. Gel left on the side while you make breakfast, every morning, for a fortnight, has spent a couple of hours a day out of the fridge. Take the spoon, put the lid on, put it back.
  3. Age. Twenty-eight days is what I print. A gel that has gone thin, sour or fizzy past that point is done. Do not push it.

If you know you will not get through a jar in a month, freeze half of it the day it arrives. A customer of mine keeps a stock in the freezer and it is exactly the right instinct. Frozen gel thaws in the fridge overnight and is as it was.

07Who should not take it

I would rather lose a sale than have you take something that is wrong for you, so read this bit properly.

  • Overactive thyroid. If you have hyperthyroidism, sea moss is not for you. It is the iodine. This is not a maybe.
  • Thyroid medication of any kind. Talk to your GP or pharmacist first, and if you do take it, take the capsules so the dose is known.
  • Pregnant or nursing. Iodine needs go up in pregnancy, and so does the importance of getting it right. Ask before you start.
  • Blood-thinning medication. Leave the Purple Mwani alone in particular. That is on its label.
  • Children. No. Keep it out of their reach.

None of this is medical advice. It is the list of people I ask questions of across the counter before I sell them a jar, and I would ask you the same.
