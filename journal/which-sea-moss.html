Sea moss · Ingredient notes

Which sea moss? Two plants, one name on the jar.

Cold-water Chondrus crispus and warm-water Eucheuma cottonii are both sold in this country as Irish moss, and they are two plants, out of two oceans, that do not look, smell or set the same. This is how we tell them apart on the bench, and which jar we would put in your hand for what.

By Reiss Davies Ausar2 September 20269 minute read

Most weeks somebody walks into 599 Smithdown Road holding a jar they bought online, pale and thick and rubbery, Irish moss written across the front of it, forty pounds or so gone, and they want to know why the jar on our counter does not look like the one in their hand. Ours is darker, looser, and it smells of low tide. Theirs, more often than not, looks like hair gel. Nobody has robbed them and nobody has quite lied to them, they have been sold one plant under the name of another, which is an older trick than any of us and still the commonest one in this trade.

The words sea moss cover several different red algae, and over the last few years the cheap one has gone out under the name of the dear one so often, on so many listings, copied off so many other listings, that most people now take the two to be one thing. They are not one thing. Life in ocean precipitates life on land, everything starts and goes back to the oceans, and the least we owe a plant that old is to call it by its own name before we konsume it. Once the two are sat side by side on a counter you sea it immediately, and after that nobody can sell you the wrong one again.

01What sea moss actually is

Sea moss is a red alga, from a group called Rhodophyta, and the Rhodophyta are among the oldest living things on this planet. They were photosynthesising in salt water long before anything had moved onto land, before there was much land worth moving onto, and every green plant on your plate today descends from something of that order. Nature is always the prerequisite design for everything man makes, and this was one of the first drawings she did. That is why we go to cold rock for it rather than to a rope in a warm lagoon.

Within that group, two species are the ones you are likely to meet in a jar in this country.

  • Chondrus crispus, the original Irish moss. Cold Atlantic water, the rocks off Ireland, Scotland, Brittany, Nova Scotia. Small flat fan-shaped fronds that fork and fork again, drying deep purple or brownish red and sometimes near black. This is the plant Irish families ate through the famine years, and the plant the old herbals mean when they write carrageen.
  • Eucheuma cottonii, the tropical one. Warm Indian Ocean and Pacific water, farmed on lines strung between stakes in shallow lagoons in Tanzania, Zanzibar, the Philippines and Indonesia. The term Mwani is Swahili for sea moss, and our team went out to the Mwani farms in Tanzania in 2020 to watch it come off the lines. The branches are thick and round, like a knobbly coral, and the colour runs pale gold through to purple. It grows fast and it costs little to produce, so most of the sea moss sold anywhere in the world is this plant.

There is a third, Gracilaria, which turns up in Caribbean gel under the name Jamaican moss, or again as Irish moss, and it is warm water, farmed and cheap like the last one. We will stay with the first two here, because those are the two on our shelf.

Most of the Irish moss sold in Britain has never been within a thousand miles of Ireland.

02How to tell them apart on the bench

We do this by eye and by smell, and you can learn to do the same in an afternoon.

Dried

  • Shape. Chondrus is flat and branching, a little like a small fern or a piece of fan coral, and the fronds are thin enough to see light through. Eucheuma is round in section, about as thick as a pencil, and looks more like a cluster of short fingers.
  • Colour. Chondrus dries dark, anywhere from purple through maroon and brown to nearly black. Eucheuma dries pale, straw and gold through to off-white, sometimes with a lilac tint. If a bag of Irish moss is golden and translucent it is NOT Chondrus, whatever the front of the bag says.
  • Smell. Wild-harvested Chondrus arrives with a fine crust of sea salt still on the fronds and smells strongly of low tide. Farmed Eucheuma is rinsed and sun-dried on tarpaulins and smells of very little. Put your nose in the bag before you put your money down.

As a gel

  • Chondrus makes a softer, looser, darker gel. Mine varies between a cranberry colour and something closer to mud, depending on the harvest.
  • Eucheuma makes a stiff, glossy, pale gel that stands up on the spoon and holds the shape of it. It is very high in carrageenan, the thickener the food industry pulls out of it by the tonne, and that is where the hair-gel look comes from.

On the label

A good label gives you the species. A label that says sea moss, or Irish moss, and stops there is usually sat on top of Eucheuma, because a seller who has paid for wild Atlantic Chondrus tends to want you to know it. Watch for pool grown and aquaculture as well. Chondrus is not farmed in pools, so a label carrying those words has already answered the question for you.

Why the price is differentWild Chondrus crispus is picked by hand off cold rock at low tide, in season, by people who know that shore and go back to it year after year. Farmed Eucheuma comes off a rope in a warm lagoon every six weeks or so. One of those costs a great deal more a kilo than the other, and the difference has to land somewhere, so it lands on the jar. A very cheap jar of Irish moss has already told you which plant is inside it.

03What is in it, and what I am allowed to say about that

Sea moss is a mineral-rich food. Iodine, magnesium, calcium, iron, zinc, selenium and several B vitamins, along with the long sugar chains that make it set. Sea vegetables screw directly into rock where the minerals are abundant, which is the whole reason they carry what they carry, and the two species we are talking about have a broadly similar profile. The differences sit in the proportions, and in how much of the jar is carrageenan.

Now I have to be plain with you about what I can and cannot say, and I would rather do it here in the middle of the page than in small print at the bottom. We run a food business in England, the only health claims we are permitted to make are the ones on the Great Britain register, those attach to a named nutrient rather than to a plant, and they are only available where we can show the product holds enough of that nutrient to qualify. For the gel we cannot show it, because the iodine in a spoonful of seaweed moves with the harvest, and a figure I cannot stand behind is worse than no figure at all. For Seaking, where every capsule is measured at 75 µg, we can. So the one sentence I am allowed on this whole page is this.

Iodine contributes to normal thyroid function and to normal energy-yielding metabolism. Two capsules of Seaking contain 150 µg, which is the adult reference intake.

Everything else you have read about this plant online is one of three things: somebody's own experience, which is theirs to describe and not mine; a claim nobody has carried to the standard the law asks for; or a claim that is illegal here and is being made anyway, usually by somebody overseas who has never stood in front of the person reading it. One sentence I can stand behind beats ten I cannot. What a spoonful does for you is yours to find out and yours to tell, and if you do find something out I would like to hear it.

04Gel or capsules

This is the question we get asked most, and the answer is really a question about what your mornings are like.

Same seaweed, two forms
GelThe whole plant, soaked and blended with water, with nothing taken out of it. It lives in the fridge, 28 days once opened, or longer in the freezer. Off a spoon, in tea, in a smoothie, or on your skin. This is the old way of doing it and it is how I take it myself.
CapsulesThe same plant dried and milled, with a measured dose in every capsule. Some people miss it and some are glad to see the back of it.

New to sea moss and at home most mornings, start with the gel. Travelling constantly, or living with a thyroid condition and needing to know exactly how much iodine has gone in, Seaking is the one.

05How I take it

Before first meal, upon rising, one tablespoon straight off the spoon, then a glass of water. That is the whole of it and I have not changed it in years.

Some people cannot get on with the texture and there is no shame in it. Stir it into a warm herbal tea with a spoonful of honey until it goes, or blend it into a smoothie, where it will thicken things a little. It goes into soup as well, blended in at the end rather than boiled.

One spoonful is the serving. If you missed yesterday, do not take two today. Seaweed carries iodine, and iodine is one of the few things on this shelf where more is a genuine problem rather than a waste of money.

The plain Chondrus gel goes on skin and hair as well. On for up to an hour, rinsed off with water, patted dry. That went on the jar because customers kept telling us they were already doing it, which is usually how these things arrive.

06Why your gel went watery, and how to store it

Three usual reasons, and this is the order to check them in.

  1. A dirty spoon. Anything that has been in your mouth, or in another jar, walks into a wet mineral-rich gel carrying company, and the gel starts coming apart within days. Clean dry spoon, every single time. It sounds fussy and it is the single biggest thing.
  2. Warmth. A jar left on the worktop while you make breakfast spends a couple of hours a day out of the fridge, and across a fortnight that is a day of warm. Take your spoonful, lid on, straight back in.
  3. Age. We print 28 days on the jar. A gel gone thin, sour or fizzy past that has had its time, and it is not worth pushing your luck on a fresh food.

If you know you will not finish a jar inside a month, freeze half of it the day it lands. One customer of ours keeps a whole shelf of it in the freezer and works through it a jar at a time, which is sensible. Frozen gel thaws overnight in the fridge and comes back fine.

07Who should not take it

I would rather lose the sale than put the wrong jar in your hand, so read this part properly.

  • Overactive thyroid. If you have hyperthyroidism, sea moss is not for you, because of the iodine.
  • Thyroid medication of any kind. Talk to your GP or pharmacist first. If they are happy for you to take it, the capsules are better because the dose is known.
  • Pregnant or nursing. Iodine needs change in pregnancy, so ask your midwife or GP before you start.
  • Blood-thinning medication. Avoid the Purple Mwani in particular, as its label says.
  • Children. It is not for them, so keep it out of their reach.

None of this is medical advice and I am not qualified to give any. It is the set of questions I put to somebody across the counter before a jar goes in a bag, and you get asked the same ones.
