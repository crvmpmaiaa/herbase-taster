What is on the inside

Nine ingredients, counted

In the order the listing declares them. Eight are plants and the ninth is a compound taken out of one. Four of the nine names identify a species; the other four are common names or a genus, and those cards say so rather than choosing a plant on the label's behalf. Where a name does not identify a species we have left the frame empty, because a photograph would be picking one.

Herb · dry ground

Tribulus terrestrisCaltrop, puncture vine

A low annual of the Zygophyllaceae that runs flat over hot dry ground from the Mediterranean through South Asia into Africa and Australia. The fruit splits into hard spined nutlets, which is where the English names come from. The listing spells the second word with one r missing. It does not say which part of the plant is used.

Tree · Southeast Asia

Eurycoma longifoliaTongkat ali

The listing gives the Malay name. It belongs to a slender understorey tree of the Simaroubaceae in the lowland forests of Malaysia, Indonesia, Thailand and Vietnam, thin enough at full height to be taken for a sapling. The root is the part that reaches the trade. The listing does not say which part is in the capsule.

Genus only · East Asia

EpimediumSpecies not named

A genus of woodland perennials in the Berberidaceae, most of them Chinese, with thin leathery leaves and spurred flowers. A genus is not an ingredient: this one holds dozens of species. Neither the species nor the part used is printed, so the frame stays empty.

Shrub · West Africa

Fadogia agrestisStem in the trade

A small shrub of the Rubiaceae from the savanna belt of West Africa, from Nigeria eastwards. The dried stem is what is sold under the name. The listing spells it agrestris; the accepted binomial is Fadogia agrestis. We found no photograph of the plant we could use, so this frame is empty as well.

Root · common name only

SarsaparillaNo binomial printed

A trade name rather than a species. It covers several climbers in the genus Smilax, and is also used for Hemidesmus indicus in India and Aralia nudicaulis in North America. The root is what the word points at in all of them. With no binomial on the label there is no way to say which plant is in the jar.

Leaf · Mexico

Turnera diffusaDamiana

A small aromatic shrub of the Passifloraceae, native to Mexico, Central America, the Caribbean and southern Texas, with toothed leaves and a small yellow flower. The leaf is the part in trade. The listing prints the common name only; the binomial above is the accepted one for it.

Pollen · genus Pinus

Pine pollenSpecies not named

Pollen shed by the male cones of pine trees and collected in spring. Pinus runs to over a hundred species and the listing does not say which one, or where it was gathered. It is also the one ingredient here that is a pollen, which matters to anyone who reacts to them.

No photographof a species the label does not name

Root · name shared

GinsengSpecies not named

Another common name doing a binomial's work. Ginseng usually means Panax ginseng from Korea and northeast China, sometimes Panax quinquefolius from North America, and on some labels Eleutherococcus senticosus, a different plant in the same family. The root is what is sold under all three. The shop sells a ginseng of its own, and this listing does not say whether it is the same plant.

Alkaloid · from pepper

PiperineNot a plant part

The one ingredient here that is not a plant or a part of one. Piperine is a compound taken out of pepper, the fruit of Piper nigrum, which is why this card shows peppercorns rather than the ingredient itself. It is also the reason for the line about prescriptions on the sheet below.

From Reiss

Nine names, and four of them are not species

A list is better than no list, and this jar has one, which already puts it ahead of half the formulas on my own shelf. Then you read it slowly. Sarsaparilla, ginseng, pine pollen, epimedium: four names that each cover more than one plant, and in two cases plants from different families. A name that covers three plants is not an ingredient, it is a category.

The other four are species and I have printed them in Latin above, spelling corrected in two places where the listing has a letter missing. Not one of the nine says which part of the plant is in the capsule. Root, leaf, stem and pollen are what the trade usually sells under these names, and usually is not a specification.

The ninth is not a plant at all. Piperine is a compound taken out of pepper, and it is the reason there is a line about prescriptions on the sheet. Anything that changes how the body takes up a medication belongs in the safety row of a page, printed plainly, whether or not it helps me sell the jar.

The listing also says this one is for men only. I have printed that because the label prints it, and I have not printed a reason, because the label does not give one and the reason would be mine rather than the manufacturer's. If you want to know more before you buy, the counter is the place for that conversation.
