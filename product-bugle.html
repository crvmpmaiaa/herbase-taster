What is on the inside

One plant, and three plants called bugle

A one ingredient list is a short section, and this one needs a paragraph about the name before anything else. Here is the plant the listing names, the plants it is not, the row the label leaves empty, and the shell.

Herb · Lamiaceae

Lycopus virginicusVirginia bugleweed

A square stemmed perennial of wet ground in eastern North America, in the mint family, with small white flowers in whorls where the leaves meet the stem. This is the plant our listing names, and it is the name that governs this page.

Not this one · Lamiaceae

Ajuga reptansBugle, in Britain

Ask for bugle in a British garden and you get Ajuga reptans, a low creeping plant with blue flower spikes, in the same family and a different genus. Lycopus europaeus, gypsywort, gets called bugleweed too. Three plants, one English word, which is why the Latin is on the sheet.

Aerial parts, usuallyThe blank on this label

The parts above ground, cut when the plant is in flower, are what is normally milled under this name. Our listing says 100 per cent of the plant and does not say which part, so this page does not state one.

From Reiss

Three plants share this name, and one of them is in the jar

If you ask for bugle in Britain you will be handed Ajuga reptans, the creeping thing with blue spikes that people grow at the front of a border. If you ask a herbalist in North America you will get Lycopus virginicus. There is a third, Lycopus europaeus, sold as gypsywort and sometimes as bugleweed. They are three different plants and only one of them is in this capsule.

Our listing names Lycopus virginicus, so that is what the sheet says and that is what the photograph on this page shows, not the garden Ajuga we were offered first and turned down. A picture of the wrong plant would have been a worse mistake than no picture at all.
