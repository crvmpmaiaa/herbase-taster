What is on the inside

Five plants, named in Latin

The listing gives a Latin ingredient line of five plants, which is more than most jars on any shelf manage. It also gives a second list in prose, in common names, which runs to four of them and adds a proprietary blend. The two do not agree. Here are the five from the Latin line, which is the one we work from. We hold a photograph of one of the five, the damiana. Brassicaceae · Peruvian Andes

Lepidium meyeniiMaca

A low cabbage-family plant grown on the Junin plateau in Peru, at altitudes above 4,000 metres where very little else is farmed. The part eaten and sold is the swollen underground storage organ, dried in the sun after lifting. The listing's prose calls it maca root, which makes it the one plant here whose part is printed.

No photographof the plant we buy

Dioscoreaceae · eastern North America

Dioscorea villosaWild yam

A twining vine of damp woodland from the eastern United States down into Mexico, with heart-shaped leaves and a knotted underground rhizome. It is not the yam of the greengrocer, which is a different set of Dioscorea species grown as a staple food. This is the plant the listing's second list leaves out.

No photographof the plant we buy

Fabaceae · the Mediterranean and western Asia

Trigonella foenum-graecumFenugreek

An annual legume with small three-part leaves and hard angular yellow seed. The seed is the part usually sold, whole or ground, and it is the fenugreek of a curry powder and of the smell people describe as maple. Being a legume is the reason for the allergen note on the sheet below.

No photographof the plant we buy

Apiaceae · the Mediterranean

Foeniculum vulgareFennel

A tall umbellifer with hair-fine leaves and flat yellow flower heads, native to the Mediterranean and naturalised on roadsides across much of the world. Both the seed and the swollen leaf base are used in kitchens. Which part is in this jar is not printed.

Passifloraceae · Mexico and the Caribbean

Turnera diffusaDamiana

A small aromatic shrub of Mexico, Central America and the West Indies, with toothed leaves and small yellow flowers. The leaf is the part usually dried and sold. Older books file the genus in a family of its own, Turneraceae, which has since been folded into Passifloraceae.

Two lists · one jar

Where they disagreeFive names against four and a blend

The Latin ingredient line names five plants and no blend. The paragraph underneath names four of them in English, drops wild yam, and adds a proprietary blend that the Latin line does not mention. One of those two lists is out of date and we cannot tell you which. The Latin line is the one this page works from, and the mismatch is printed here rather than tidied away.

Reiss's turn

Two lists, and neither of them is wrong on purpose

This listing does something almost no jar on any shelf does: it names five plants in Latin, on the ingredient line, before it says anything else. That line is the reason this page exists in the shape it does. You can take any one of those five names, look it up in a flora, and check it.

Then, four paragraphs down, there is a second list in English that names four of the five and adds a proprietary blend. Wild yam has gone. I do not know which of the two lists came first, and I am not going to guess in public, so both are on the sheet with the disagreement marked.

The fix is a printing job rather than a reformulation, and it is on the list for the next label. Until then, the Latin line is what this page works from, because a binomial is checkable and an English name is a nickname.

The fenugreek note on the sheet is the one thing on this page I would not want anybody to skip. It is a legume, in with peanut and chickpea, and that matters to some people regardless of anything else.

A binomial is checkable. An English name is a nickname.
