What is on the inside

One plant, named

There is one botanical on this listing and there is no second one. Here is the species, what the label says about the material in the capsule, and the two places where it says nothing.

Herb · Phyllanthaceae

Phyllanthus niruriChanca piedra

An annual herb of the wet tropics, rarely more than 60 cm tall. The small oblong leaves sit in two ranks along each branchlet, so a whole branch reads at a glance like one pinnate leaf. The tiny flowers and the round seed capsules hang underneath the branchlet rather than above it, which is the quickest way to place the genus in the field. It grows across South America, the Caribbean, West Africa and South Asia.

Reiss's turn

Why this page leaves the name alone

Chanca piedra is what the plant is called in Peru and in Brazil, and the name has a plain meaning in Spanish. I am not going to print that meaning, because the meaning is a claim, and a claim in another language is still a claim. The name goes on the jar as a name. That is the whole of the work it does here.

What I can print is short. One plant, named in Latin. Eight hundred milligrams in a capsule. Two capsules in the morning, two in the evening, a big glass of water with each pair. Everything else on the sheet is a line about what the label does not tell me, and the one that annoys me most is the capsule count. Until that number is printed I cannot give you a cost a day, and I would rather leave the line blank than round one up and hope.

A herb this widely used should have more printed on it than this. The reason it does not is that it arrived in the trade as a tradition rather than as a specification. Traditions travel well and cost nothing to carry. Numbers have to be measured, and measuring costs money that a shop this size spends one label at a time.

A claim in another language is still a claim.
