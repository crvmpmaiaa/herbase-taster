What is on the inside

Five plants, named in Latin

The listing prints a Latin ingredient line of five plants. It does not say whether those five are all of them, and it does not name the part used for any of them. Here is what each plant is, where it grows, and which part of it the trade usually sells. We hold no photograph of any of the five, so those frames are empty rather than filled with a stand-in, and any picture on this page that is a generated scene says so.

Smilacaceae · Central and South America

Smilax officinalisSarsaparilla

A woody climbing vine of Costa Rica, Colombia and Ecuador that scrambles through forest on prickly stems. The long root and rhizome are the part traded. Sarsaparilla is a trade name that covers several Smilax species rather than one, and this listing names one of them, which is more than most sarsaparilla on sale manages.

No photographof the plant we buy

Asteraceae · Eurasia

Arctium lappaBurdock

A tall biennial of Eurasian roadsides and waste ground, in the daisy family, with big rhubarb-like leaves in the first year and hooked burrs in the second. Those burrs, caught in a dog's coat, are what gave a Swiss engineer the idea for hook and loop fastening. The root is the part usually sold, in Japan as gobo, and it is the burdock of dandelion and burdock. The listing spells the genus Articum; the accepted spelling is Arctium.

No photographof the plant we buy

Polygonaceae · Europe, now worldwide

Rumex crispusYellow dock

A deep-rooted perennial weed of European field edges, now naturalised almost everywhere, named for the crimped wavy margin of its leaves. It carries rust-brown seed spikes that stand through the winter. The root is the part usually sold. It is a plant high in oxalate, which is the reason for the note on the sheet below.

No photographof the plant we buy

Malvaceae · sub-Saharan Africa

Adansonia digitataBaobab

The broad-trunked African tree with branches that look like roots in the air. Its fruit hangs in a hard velvety pod and the pulp inside dries on the tree by itself, so it is milled to a pale powder without any drying step at all. Which is unusual enough to be worth saying about a plant.

No photographof the plant we buy

Caryophyllaceae · temperate worldwide

Stellaria mediaChickweed

A low sprawling annual of gardens, allotments and waste ground across the temperate world, with a single line of hairs running up one side of the stem and tiny white flowers whose five deeply split petals look like ten. The whole plant above ground is the part usually gathered.

Reiss's turn

Why the mineral lines came off this page

The old listing for this jar states that it contains iron, zinc and B vitamins, and then says what each of those is meant to do. Both halves came off. Not because the plants are wrong, but because nobody has measured this jar. Without a number from a test, a mineral sentence is decoration, and decoration is exactly what I do not want on a specification sheet.

The one place on this whole site where a mineral figure survives is Seaking, which is the one place a per capsule figure is printed at all. Whether an assay sits behind it is not stated on that listing either, so we do not say it does. That is the standard: a number, printed on the formula it describes, not borrowed from a generic list. When this jar has one, it goes on the sheet the same day.

There is a second thing worth correcting in the open. The listing's prose talks about dandelion and burdock. Burdock, Arctium lappa, is on the ingredient line. Dandelion is not on it at all. Somebody wrote a good sentence and nobody checked it against the list underneath.

The Latin is misspelled on the listing as well, Articum for Arctium. It is a small thing. It is also the kind of small thing that tells you whether anyone checked, so it is corrected on the sheet above with the change marked rather than done quietly.

A good sentence is not an ingredient.
